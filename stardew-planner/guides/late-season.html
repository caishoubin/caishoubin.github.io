Field note 03 · Before the season turns

Regrowing doesn’t always mean rewarding.

A crop can survive long enough to produce and still fail to pay for its seed. Late-season planning needs two checks: time to harvest and time to earn back the purchase.

Last reviewed October 20, 2026 · Independent fan resource

Regrowing crops are appealing because one seed can produce repeatedly. The upfront cost is spread over several harvests. But the calendar does not promise those harvests. Buy the same seed late in the season and you may pay the full price for only a small part of its potential.

The cranberry example

A cranberry seed costs 240g at Pierre’s standard price. The first harvest takes seven days and has a minimum yield of two cranberries. Each normal-quality cranberry sells raw for 75g. After that, the plant regrows every five days.

Plant on Fall 21 and your first harvest arrives on Fall 28. There is no room for another harvest in that outdoor season. Minimum raw sales per planted tile are two berries × 75g = 150g. Subtract the 240g seed and the seed-only net is −90g per tile.

A harvest is not the same as a profit. This planting meets the crop’s final deadline, but its minimum base-quality raw sales do not recover the standard seed cost.

With a 500g budget and 20 plots, that late planting buys two seeds for 480g. You retain 20g, harvest four berries worth 300g, and end up with 320g after sales. Compared with the 500g you started with, the modeled loss is 180g. Extra random berries or quality bonuses could alter the real result, but they are not included here.

Count harvest dates, not just regrowth speed

A crop described as “every five days” does not give you a harvest five days after planting. It must finish its initial growth first. Cranberries planted on Fall 1 first produce on Fall 8, then on Fall 13, 18, 23, and 28. That is a very different calendar from the Fall 21 planting.

Blueberries offer another clear illustration. Their initial growth is thirteen days, followed by four-day regrowth. Planted on Summer 1, they harvest on Summer 14, 18, 22, and 26. Planted on Summer 15, the last possible day at normal speed, they harvest only on Summer 28.

That late blueberry harvest still produces at least three berries. At 50g each, its 150g raw revenue exceeds the standard 80g seed price by 70g per tile. So “late regrowing crops are always bad” is also the wrong lesson. Check each crop’s upfront price, first yield, and remaining dates.

What a faster crop can and cannot fix

A short growth time can make a crop fit where a slow one cannot. Fall bok choy takes four days, so a Fall 24 planting is ready on Fall 28. A pumpkin needs thirteen days, making Fall 15 its normal-speed deadline.

Do not assume the faster crop is universally better. The choice also depends on seeds you can afford, available space, raw sale prices, and your purpose. You may be growing an item for a bundle, recipe, gift, or another goal rather than maximizing gold. Last Seed only calculates the stated raw-sale plan; it does not assign a value to those other uses.

Three questions before the seed purchase

  1. Does the first harvest fit? Add the initial growth days to today’s date. A result after day 28 means this selected outdoor crop is too late.
  2. How many harvests actually fit? Add each regrowth interval to the first harvest date. Stop at day 28, including a harvest that lands exactly on it.
  3. Do the resulting sales justify the seed cost for my goal? Compare gross raw sales with spending, and consider the cash you need before harvest.

Leaving a plot empty is a valid plan

An empty tile is not automatically a mistake. If no crop in your intended plan can finish, buying a seed does not create a return. Keeping gold available can leave you better prepared for the next season. This tool does not model every alternative activity or every crop, so its small catalog should not be treated as a complete list of what your farm can do.

Late-season farming is mostly about being honest with the calendar. A modest plan that finishes is easier to evaluate than an impressive-looking revenue figure built on harvests you do not have time to collect.
