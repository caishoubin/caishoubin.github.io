Last Seed/Field note 02 · Your seed money
Field note 02 · Your seed money

A small budget can grow good things.

“What is the best crop?” becomes a much more useful question when you add three details: your gold, your available plots, and the date.

Last reviewed October 20, 2026 · Independent fan resource

A crop can return more per tile and still be awkward for a cash-poor farm. Another can return less per harvest but pay you sooner. Seed cost, growth time, and growing room pull your decision in different directions. Last Seed makes those trade-offs visible without pretending to solve every possible season.

Start by separating three numbers

Seed spending is the gold you commit now. Gross sales is the value of the harvested raw crops. Net after seed cost is gross sales minus the purchase of seeds. Calling all three “profit” makes comparisons confusing.

There is a fourth number worth noticing: cash left after seeds. This is money you can still use before the first harvest. It is not profit, and the tool does not add future crop income to it. If you need gold for another purchase before the crops mature, this figure may decide your planting.

A worked example: 500g and 20 plots

Suppose it is Spring 1. You have 500g for seeds and 20 usable tiles. You plan one initial sowing, water every day, and sell only normal-quality raw produce. Here is what three crops do under those same assumptions.

Parsnips: fill the space, keep some cash

At 20g per seed, your budget could buy 25 parsnip seeds, but you have room for only 20. Planting 20 costs 400g, leaving 100g. Four days later, on Spring 5, the minimum harvest is 20 parsnips. At 35g each, gross sales are 700g and seed-only net is 300g.

After selling, your starting 500g has become 800g: the 100g you kept plus the 700g harvest. That is not 800g of profit. The gain over your starting money is 300g.

Potatoes: the same modeled gain, fewer plants

At 50g per seed, 500g buys 10 potato seeds. Half of your 20 plots remain unplanted, and your seed budget is fully committed. Six growth days put the harvest on Spring 7. Ten base-quality potatoes at 80g each give 800g gross sales and 300g seed-only net.

Real potato harvests can produce extra potatoes, but this planner deliberately uses the minimum yield of one. The comparison should therefore be read as a conservative crop-yield calculation, not an exact prediction of every harvest.

Cauliflower: a larger return with a longer wait

At 80g per seed, you can buy six cauliflower seeds for 480g. You keep 20g and leave 14 plots unplanted. The twelve-day growth time puts the harvest on Spring 13. Six crops at 175g each give 1,050g gross sales and 570g seed-only net.

That is the largest one-sowing net among these three examples. It also ties up most of your gold for longer. If your immediate aim is another purchase or an earlier round of seed buying, the timing may matter more than the largest number.

The hidden comparison trap: one cauliflower harvest and one parsnip harvest do not use the same amount of time. Replanting the faster crop can change the season-long result. Last Seed does not simulate that extra cycle or reinvest its proceeds.

Use your bottleneck to choose the comparison

  • Short on gold? Look at how many seeds you can afford and how much cash remains.
  • Short on space? A higher return per occupied tile can matter, but only if you can afford the seeds and finish the crop.
  • Short on days? Check the first harvest date before comparing revenue.
  • Short on watering capacity? A larger plant count also means a larger daily task. Time and energy are not priced in the calculator.

Change the date before taking the answer with you

Move the same example to Spring 20 and cauliflower cannot finish by the end of the season. Parsnips and potatoes still can. A crop’s seed price and sale value have not changed, but its usefulness has. This is why a budget comparison without a planting date is incomplete.

Run the numbers for the farm you actually have today. Then decide how much flexibility you want to keep for tomorrow.
