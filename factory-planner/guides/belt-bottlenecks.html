NOTE 001 / TRANSPORT

Find and fix Satisfactory belt bottlenecks

One short, slow conveyor can hold back an entire production line. Diagnose the route before adding more machines.

INDEPENDENT FAN TOOL · SOLID-ITEM LOGISTICS · UPDATED 21 OCT 2026

Start with a simple question: how many items need to pass through this exact connection every minute? A factory-wide total is useful for planning, but a bottleneck lives on a particular segment. Draw the route from producer to consumer, mark the flow on each branch and check each segment against its own capacity.

1. Follow the slowest serial segment

Conveyors connected in series do not add their capacities. The slowest segment places an upper bound on the whole route. A Mk.5 belt leading into a short Mk.2 belt and then another Mk.5 belt is still limited to 120 items per minute across that connection. Check lifts and short pieces close to machines as well as the long, obvious runs.

Route ceiling = minimum capacity along the route
Sustainable input = min(available production, route ceiling)

Worked example: a 600-item route

You have 600 items/min of available production and consumers asking for 600 items/min. One Mk.4 belt carries at most 480 items/min. The sustainable delivery is 480, leaving a deficit of 120 items/min.

Another production machine will not increase delivery through that belt. A Mk.5 route has a 780-item/min ceiling, so it has enough transport capacity for the same demand, provided no slower segment remains.

2. Add flows where they merge

A merger does not create capacity. If three branches each bring 180 items/min, their combined requested flow is 540 items/min. A Mk.4 exit cannot carry all 540. Its load would be 540 ÷ 480 = 112.5%, which means the requested flow exceeds the rating, not that the belt will actually operate faster.

Inspect the belt immediately after each merger. Upstream branches can look healthy while the shared exit throttles them. When branches have different destinations, keeping them separate may be simpler than merging everything into a single trunk and splitting it again.

3. Treat parallel belts as independent routes

Minimum parallel belts = ceil(target flow ÷ belt capacity)

For 900 items/min on Mk.4 belts, the minimum is ceil(900 ÷ 480) = 2. An even distribution would send 450 items/min down each lane. This is a capacity calculation; it does not automatically design the splitter arrangement.

Do not merge the two lanes back into one undersized exit. Two Mk.4 belts can provide 960 items/min of aggregate capacity only while the full route preserves that capacity. A single Mk.4 segment after the merge recreates the original 480-item/min limit.

4. Separate a capacity problem from a supply problem

If the machines can supply only 240 items/min while demand is 360, a 480-capacity belt is already sufficient for the available production. The remaining 120-item/min shortage comes from supply. Check whether upstream machines have their required ingredients, power and configured production rate. Use observed sustained flow when nominal recipe output is not being reached.

Compare tiers without mixing the questions

The workbench compares every tier using the same inputs. “Routes for target” answers how many independent belts would carry your chosen target flow. “One-route delivery” and “Unmet demand” instead use the actual production and demand on just one route. These can differ when the target does not equal demand.

For 600 items/min, the lowest sufficient single-belt tier is Mk.5. Two Mk.4 routes also have enough combined capacity, but only if the full path can distribute and receive the flow. If production is only 240 items/min against 360 demand, neither change creates the missing 120 items/min.

A short inspection checklist

  1. Write down available supply and total consumer demand for this route.
  2. Check every serial belt segment and lift, including pieces hidden behind splitters.
  3. Sum branch flows immediately after mergers.
  4. For parallel routes, check distribution and capacity all the way to the consumers.
  5. Recheck after startup has settled. A manifold filling up can temporarily look different from its steady state.

Running at exactly the rated capacity is valid in this ideal model. Extra headroom is a planning choice for growth and uncertainty, not a secret in-game throughput rule. The calculator does not simulate machine cycles, splitter behavior or transient startup.
